Sleep-Time Compute

Jun 23 2025 · 4 min read · Shubham

So here's the thing: we've gotten used to scaling compute at the exact moment we ask a question to a large language model. This is what people call test-time compute - throw in the context, add the query, and then spin up some big model to do its thing. Sure, it works well for tough problems. But it also burns a ton of compute and time. And honestly, it's a bit wasteful.

Why? Because this whole setup assumes that every problem is stateless - that you only bring the background context at the moment of the query. No memory. No persistence. Every time you ask something, it's like Groundhog Day: the model re-reads and re-reasons from scratch.

But in reality? Most real-world LLM apps are stateful. Think of a chatbot trained on your company's docs. Or a coding assistant that knows your repo. These aren't one-shot tasks. The context doesn't change much between queries. Yet we're still burning compute to reprocess the same stuff again and again.

Enter Sleep-Time Compute

Here's the idea: instead of waking the model up only when the user sends a query, and then throwing all the context at it in real-time, why not do some of the heavy lifting before the question is even asked?

That's sleep-time compute. The model gets some downtime (or low-priority background cycles) to pre-digest the context ahead of time. Then, when the actual query comes in, the model already has a semantically enriched, pre-processed version of the context ready to go.

So instead of the standard approach:

$$T_B(q, c) \rightarrow a$$

where $T$ is the model, $B$ is a big compute budget, $q$ is the query, $c$ is the raw context, and $a$ is the answer... we shift to this:

$$S(c) \rightarrow c'$$ $$T_b(q, c') \rightarrow a$$

Where $S(c)$ is the sleep-time step, transforming the raw context $c$ into a richer representation $c'$. Then $T$ uses a much smaller budget $b$ at test-time, because most of the reasoning was frontloaded.

Does It Actually Work?

Turns out: yes, but not always.

Sleep-time compute works best when the questions are predictable from the context. Like when you're working inside a domain where the queries follow known patterns - customer support knowledge bases, technical documentation, etc. In these cases, a good chunk of the reasoning can be done ahead of time.

Experiments show that as query predictability increases, the gap between sleep-time and full test-time compute grows - in sleep-time's favor. Better performance and lower latency without spinning up giant models every time someone types a question.

Final Thought

Models don't just answer questions - they prepare for them.

And honestly, that feels like a smarter way to use machine time.
